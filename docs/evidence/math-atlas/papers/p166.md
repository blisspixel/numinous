## The mathematical question

A directional Hilbert transform is claimed uniformly bounded when integration length is tied to the reciprocal Lipschitz constant.

## What makes it hard

Integration is along the line direction frozen at the evaluation point, not necessarily along a curved streamline. The scale restriction is essential.

## Inside the construction

### finite-combinatorics

Separated frequencies make every triple have at most one completion to a near-additive quadruple; the source sums weighted quadruples to control a fourth-moment quantity.

- **Source locator:** phase:quantile-gap
- **Numinous use:** A frequency constellation can display near-resonant quadruples and an exact combinatorial count for rational inputs.
- **Limit:** Approximate-equality thresholds and frequency separation must be explicit; a graph of resonances alone is not the directional Hilbert theorem.

### quantitative-boundary

The finite Fourier gain distinguishes a dominant coefficient from a strict deficit; its improved exponent contains the tiny constant 2^-60 divided by log(2D).

- **Source locator:** phase:parameters; phase:quantitative-gap
- **Numinous use:** Show coefficient concentration and the size of the available theoretical gain alongside measured finite moments.
- **Limit:** The tiny guaranteed improvement may be below useful numerical resolution and should not be marketed as a practical speedup.
