## The mathematical question

The second-neighborhood manuscript claims every oriented graph has a vertex whose distance-two neighborhood is at least its out-neighborhood.

## Visual and sonic possibility

Two colored reachability waves make the comparison immediate; wave counts may map to notes.

## What makes it hard

Oriented graphs forbid opposite arcs, and distance exactly two excludes first neighbors and the source. Counting walks instead of distinct vertices would silently change the mathematical object.

## Inside the construction

### invariant

F(S) is the union of out-neighborhoods and may overlap S; the deficit uses explicit set differences rather than disjoint layers.

- **Source locator:** eq:subset-deficit
- **Numinous use:** Compare one-step and two-step neighborhood sets.
- **Limit:** Confusing F squared with the strict second neighborhood changes the claim.

### construction

A minimal hypothetical counterexample is expanded by transitive tournaments to obtain a strict inequality for every nonempty proper subset.

- **Source locator:** prop:counterexample-reduction
- **Numinous use:** Check subset inequalities exhaustively in small oriented graphs.
- **Limit:** The construction is conditional on a counterexample, not an example supplied by the theorem.
